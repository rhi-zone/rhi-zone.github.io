# Omnimodal media

Computers can do literally anything. The status quo is virtual paper, virtual music, virtual photos — we took physical media, digitized them, and stopped. The "virtual" prefix didn't unlock anything new, it just removed the manufacturing cost. The computer is a printing press, a record player, a photo album, a movie projector, a mailbox — all metaphors from before it existed. Limiting to static anything feels like leaving stuff on the table.

## The word

"Multimedia" means a single artifact that combines multiple media types — text, images, video in one container. "Multimodal" describes a capability: this thing handles multiple modes. "Omnimodal" is different from both, not in a hard-requirement way but in intent. It's not a checklist of supported modalities. It's an orientation — the work isn't organized around any particular mode. Not a property of composition (what's inside the artifact), but a posture toward what the artifact even is.

## The modular synth thing

Modular synths decompose sound into its actual primitives — oscillators, filters, envelopes, LFOs — and let you route them however you want. There's no built-in assumption about what a "note" is or what an "instrument" sounds like. The blinders that traditional instruments impose (a piano has a keyboard, has a fixed timbre, produces discrete pitches) are gone. You're working with the raw material.

The point isn't that synths found a "native electronic voice" or that they made electronic music accessible. It's that they removed blinders. They stopped assuming the output had to resemble something from before.

Interactive experiences haven't been decomposed into their primitives yet. We're still working at the level of "game" and "website" and "app" — pre-assembled instruments — instead of the raw material underneath. Games imitate board games or sports or cinema. "Interactive fiction" is still fiction. Web art is still art-on-a-screen. Even the most experimental stuff tends to be a known form with interactivity bolted on.

## Breaking the medium

House of Leaves does a version of this in print — it uses the book against itself, making the physical form part of the content rather than a transparent container. The medium stops being invisible and becomes material.

On a computer, "breaking the medium" is interesting because there's no fixed medium to break — the container is infinitely malleable. But there's everything to push against: people expect a webpage to scroll vertically, expect audio to play linearly, expect a cursor to be a pointer, expect a click to navigate. Decades of UI convention have built a form anyway, and the constraints are all arbitrary and everyone knows it. That's more to push against, not less.

## What's unnamed

What does a creative work look like when it starts from the computer's actual capabilities instead of from a pre-digital form? There are games, there are lots of interesting interactive experiences, but the thing that isn't any of those — not a game (no win condition necessarily), not a film (not linear or passive), not an album (not purely audio), not an app (not utilitarian) — doesn't have a name yet.

Maybe it doesn't need one yet. But the gap is real: the creative works that could only exist as computational experiences, that aren't imitating a prior form, are undersupplied relative to what the medium can do.
