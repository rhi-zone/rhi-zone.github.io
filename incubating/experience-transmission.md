# Making someone feel an experience they've never had

The question: how do you make someone feel an experience they've never had, through static media — mostly prose, but not only prose? The case that started this was gender dysphoria, but the problem is general. Any experience where not-knowing causes harm fits: exploitation, addiction, disability, the whole list.

## A rough scale

Three points that seem solid enough to anchor on:

- 0%: total ignorance.
- About 1%: explanation. Articles, documentaries, testimonials, someone telling you what it's like. This barely moves the needle.
- 100%: actually living it. Being trans, say.

What's striking is how little ground explanation covers. You'd expect being told about something to at least get you partway there, but the line from "told about it" to "understands it" is nearly flat, and then there's this huge jump to 100 at lived experience. The gap between "knows the facts" and "gets it" is enormous, and most attempts to close it — the article, the PSA, the awareness campaign — barely touch it.

## What allies suggest

Allies get somewhere real without living the experience themselves, so they're a useful data point for what's on the other side of that 1% ceiling. The mechanism looks relational: proximity over time to someone who lives it, caring about a specific person, watching the day-to-day reality unfold rather than being told about it in one sitting. It's slow and it's involuntary — nobody signs up for "I am now going to develop empathy over the next three years by watching my friend go through this." It just happens as a side effect of the relationship.

That suggests witnessing beats being-told, and duration matters, and the specific-person part matters. None of those are things a one-off article can offer, but they might be things a piece of media can approximate.

## What screens can do that prose alone can't

Prose gives you witnessing at best — you're told about a character's inner life. Screens (games especially) can do first-person POV: the player isn't watching someone else go through it, it's happening to them. That's a different mechanism than the ally one — not proximity-over-time to another person, but direct simulated experience. Whether that's actually stronger or just different is open; it might depend on the experience.

## The decompressor model

Here's a way of thinking about why explanation is so weak. Words are compressed experience — the author encodes something into text, and the reader's brain decompresses it back into something felt, using material already in their head: their own memories, associations, prior experiences. The problem is everyone's decompressor is built differently, out of different raw material, so the same sentence decompresses into wildly different things for different readers. There's no universal shared core you can target, because the overlap between any two people's decompressors isn't the same as the overlap between any other two.

This seems to explain the 1% ceiling. A short factual description assumes a decompressor the reader doesn't have, so it comes out flat no matter how accurate the words are.

The interesting move, though: you can't rewrite the encoding per-reader (it's static media, one text for everyone), but the story itself can reshape the reader's decompressor before it delivers the payload. This is arguably what good storytelling already does. A novel spends two hundred pages building the machinery you need to feel the climax the way it's meant to be felt — establishing what a character values, what loss would mean to them, what the reader should have come to want by page 200. The setup isn't preamble to the point, it IS the work. The concept itself, if you tried to state it directly, might be one sentence. Everything else is decompressor-building.

So maybe the target isn't "convey the experience" — it's "spend the whole piece building the reader a decompressor capable of receiving the experience," and only then deliver it.

## One game idea: investment then swap

One direction, specific to the dysphoria case: get the player deeply invested in a character they built themselves, then forcibly place that character in the wrong body. The world keeps acting like nothing changed, because that IS the actual experience being modeled — the wrongness is only visible to the person feeling it, invisible to everyone around them.

A variant that uses the player's own face: apply a gender-swap filter to a live camera feed. This is the one version that doesn't need a fictional proxy at all — it's literally your face, made wrong. It also raises real problems: privacy, consent, and the fact that reactions are unpredictable. There's also an odd possibility worth naming rather than dismissing — some players might respond by liking what they see, and end up learning something about themselves they didn't expect from a piece meant to build empathy for someone else. Not necessarily a failure mode, just a genuinely open outcome of that particular mechanic.

## The aphantasia parallel

People often "know" they're trans before they know they're trans, in something like the way people with aphantasia don't realize they can't visualize things until they find out other people can — you can't miss what you don't know exists as a category. If the swap mechanic gets used, this suggests it should land suddenly rather than build gradually: the wrongness was there the whole time, and what changes is that the player suddenly has the context to feel it, not that the wrongness itself grows.

## Why doesn't this already exist

A few structural reasons this seems undersupplied:

The people who actually have the experience have little incentive to spend the emotional labor building empathy tools for the people who hurt them — and doing it well would mean reliving it, which is its own cost. When trans people do write about this, the writing is naturally optimized for what they need: being seen, being understood, not being alone. That's a real and legitimate purpose, but it's a different purpose from transmission to a reader with an unrelated decompressor. Expression and transmission aren't the same target.

Separately, the people who most need to understand this are the least likely to go looking for it. Nobody who isn't already curious opens an article titled "understanding gender dysphoria."

And there's no market pull — there's no obvious audience clamoring for "a thing designed to make you feel bad so you understand something you weren't asking to understand."

The audience problem has a fairly trivial workaround, though: don't tell people what it's about. Make a good story or game that people engage with on its own merits, and let the experience ride along without being advertised as the point.

## Prior art: Metamorphosis (177013)

A hentai manga by ShindoL, a single oneshot following a girl's descent into exploitation. It hits harder than you'd expect from something in that category, and it's worth looking at for what it does well:

No blame — the trap is structural, not a flaw in the character, so the reader can't get distance by judging her for making a bad choice. An ordinary starting point, which makes identification easy — there's a low barrier to seeing yourself in her. And it never moralizes; it doesn't tell you how to feel about what's happening, it just shows it.

Where it seems to fall short of lasting change: the reader has no reference point for what's actually happening to her, so they can feel bad about it without feeling it, if that distinction makes sense — the emotional reaction is real but it isn't grounded in anything from the reader's own life, so there's no hook for it to attach to and persist. There's also the fact that the community turned it into a meme, which reframes the whole thing as entertainment and probably undoes a lot of what the piece was doing. Still useful as an existence proof for what a single piece of static fiction can pull off within tight constraints.

## What research says separates "felt something" from "changed permanently"

There's a cluster of properties that show up across studies of narrative persuasion and attitude change, and they seem to stack — fiction that actually changes minds tends to hit most of them, fiction that doesn't tends to hit one or two.

Don't announce the persuasive intent. If the reader can see the story is trying to convince them of something, they push back — attitudes have been measured shifting 10-20% away from the intended direction. The story can't read as a message with a plot wrapped around it.

Particularize before generalizing. One named person lands harder than a statistic or a description of a group — and past a point, adding more suffering actually reduces compassion rather than increasing it (compassion fade is the term for this).

Let the reader arrive at the conclusion themselves. A conclusion the reader works out on their own seems to resist erosion better than one that's handed to them, probably because self-derived understanding doesn't trip the "someone is persuading me" alarm the way delivered conclusions do.

Leave something portable behind. The most durable mind-changes seem to be ones that leave a vocabulary or a concept behind — "Orwellian," "doublethink," "catch-22" — words that outlive the story and get reapplied to new situations later. Feelings fade fast; a new category for perceiving things doesn't.

Land on ground that's already primed. A single exposure rarely rewires anyone on its own. The famous cases — Uncle Tom's Cabin, The Jungle — worked because there was already political pressure building; the book gave people who were already halfway there a place to land.

Immersion and technology aren't a substitute for the above. VR "empathy machines" seem to improve emotional empathy (feeling something in the moment) without improving cognitive empathy (actually understanding the other person's situation). High fidelity without character depth gets you sympathy that doesn't outlast the session.

Worth flagging clearly: these are observations pulled out of a messy, complicated phenomenon, not a recipe. If you optimize for hitting all six as targets, you risk producing something that has all the surface features of persuasive fiction without doing the actual thing — Goodhart's law applied to storytelling. They're probably better held as constraints ("don't do the opposite of these") than as objectives to chase directly. Any list of principles pulled out of something this complex is an incomplete map, and there's real texture in the territory that no list like this captures.

One more thing worth naming separately: form-as-content. Flowers for Algernon degrades its own prose as the narrator's mind degrades — the reading experience itself gets harder and sadder in a way that mirrors what's happening to the character. That's about as close as fiction gets to simulating an experience rather than describing one, and it might be a whole approach on its own rather than just one item on the list above.

## Open questions

What format to start with is genuinely open. Prose is the most conservative choice — it's just writing, no new tools or team needed. Multiple formats eventually makes sense, since different formats probably reach different audiences and might do different parts of the job (witnessing vs. first-person, say), but that's a later problem.

Genre is undecided, and it's not obvious genre is even the right question to lead with — it might follow from other decisions rather than come first.

The "portable artifact" finding is the one that nags. Is there a word or concept for dysphoria that could click the way "Orwellian" clicked — something that gives people a permanent new category rather than just a temporary feeling? Not clear this is something you can design on purpose versus something that either happens or doesn't.

How this relates to "existence" (the text-based HTML5 game in ~/git/paragarden/existence, a power anti-fantasy) is worth asking. Existence is more of a sandbox/sim, which is probably too unstructured for something that needs to reshape the reader's decompressor in a specific sequence before delivering a specific payload — that kind of pacing wants more authorial control than a sandbox gives you. Likely a separate project, at least for now, but worth revisiting if the shape of either one changes.

The Enigma of Amigara Fault got flagged as relevant prior art without anyone pinning down exactly why yet. Something about the holes shaped exactly like each person, and the fact that once you've seen the one that's yours, you can't not go into it. There's something in that — compulsion, a body that already fits a shape before you understand why — that seems to rhyme with this problem, but it hasn't been worked out yet.

## Dropped threads and nuances

A few threads that shaped the thinking but didn't make it into the main doc.

**Identification vs. observation, and the dignity tradeoff.** The doc mentions that screens can do first-person POV (the player experiencing it directly) versus prose doing witnessing at best. What didn't get fully spelled out: identification is more visceral, but observation preserves the dignity and agency of the person being observed. It's not a flat hierarchy where identification is strictly better — it's a real tradeoff depending on what you're trying to do and who you're doing it for. The swap mechanic tips toward identification; watching a character narrate their experience tips toward observation. Both are legitimate moves.

**The author problem, sharpened.** The doc frames it as "little incentive" for people with lived experience to build empathy tools. Sharper: you're asking someone to do emotional labor to build understanding *for the group that hurt them*. It's not just a lack of incentive, it's actively asking for work that beneficiates an oppressor. That's a different kind of barrier than just "market doesn't pull for it."

**Why vent art isn't transmission.** When people with an experience produce art, they're naturally optimized for internal recognition — being seen, being understood, not being alone. That's expression, which serves them. Vent art is raw, unmediated, assumes a decompressor the audience already has. This is exactly why it doesn't transmit to someone without the experience: the author isn't building a decompressor, they're venting to one that already exists. This is a structural reason, not a failure of craft. It explains why the art that resonates hardest with people inside an experience often doesn't move people outside it.

**Bond before ideology.** Research on cult radicalization and emotional bonding found that effective ideological shifts happen when emotional bonding comes first, then content second. This is the inverse of the didactic failure mode (facts and arguments delivered before there's any reason to care). It's why the "lie" approach matters — you make someone care about the story or character first, and the understanding lands second. Once there's emotional investment, ideological updates happen more readily. It's also why announcing the persuasive intent kills the whole thing: the moment the reader sees "this story is trying to convince me," the bond mechanism shuts down.

**Parasocial contact and stereotype updating.** A related finding: people who enjoy a bond with a character from an outgroup experience cognitive dissonance against their prior stereotype. They resolve this by updating the stereotype (that outgroup member is "not like the others" gets replaced with a broader category update) rather than discounting the character as an exception. It's a mechanism for how stories actually change minds — not by arguing against the stereotype, but by making the person care about someone the stereotype supposedly applies to, which forces a recategorization. The story doesn't need to make the case; the person's own brain does the work.

**Dysphoria was the case, but the problem is general.** The framing throughout the doc is sometimes specific to dysphoria, but the conversation started from "how do you convey concepts as natively as possible to the human brain" — it's really about native cognition and compression gaps, not dysphoria specifically. Any experience where ignorance causes harm fits the pattern. Dysphoria was just the concrete entry point.

**The "portable artifact" finding struck as non-obvious.** The doc flags it as the one that nags, which is fair, but it didn't capture what made it worth flagging in the first place: it was surprising, a finding that didn't follow obviously from the prior reasoning. Worth sitting with for that reason alone, not necessarily as the answer to the problem.
